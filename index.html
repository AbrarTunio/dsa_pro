Lesson 1 of 3

Probability fundamentals and key distributions

A probability is the long-run share of times something happens. A distribution lists every possible outcome with its probability. You will meet the three you will use most: binomial for counting successes, Poisson for counting random arrivals, and normal for measurements.

Missions

    1.1

    Coins, dice and the law of large numbers

    In simple words

    If you repeat something random many times, the results average out to the true chance. One coin flip tells you nothing, but a thousand flips show you the coin lands heads about half the time.

    One flip is unpredictable. The share of heads over many flips is not. Flip a handful and the proportion jumps around; flip hundreds and it settles on the true probability.

    Where you see it

    Insurers can’t predict whether you will crash this year, but across a million drivers the crash rate is stable enough to price a policy. Casinos, airlines overbooking seats, and cricket analysts all rely on this law.

    What to notice

    Early on, the gap from the truth is large. It shrinks roughly like 1/√n, so going from 100 to 400 flips only halves the typical error.
    1.2

    Binomial: how many customers will buy?

    In simple words

    The binomial distribution counts how many successes you get from a fixed number of tries, when every try has the same chance. Example: out of 20 customers, each with a 30% chance of buying, how many will actually buy?

    A phone shop gets n walk-in customers a day. Each one buys with probability p, independently of the others. The binomial distribution tells the owner how likely each number of sales is.

    –exactly k sales
    –k or more sales
    –expected sales (np)

    Dark bar: exactly k. Mid-blue bars: more than k. Hover a bar for its probability.

    Where you see it

    Defective items in a batch, patients who respond to a drug, correct answers when guessing an MCQ test, or how many of 300 booked passengers actually turn up.

    What to notice

    At p = 0.5 the shape is perfectly symmetric. Push p towards 0 or 1 and it leans. Increase n and it starts to look like a bell, exactly like the board at the top.
    1.3

    Poisson: will the help desk be swamped?

    In simple words

    The Poisson distribution counts how many random events happen in a fixed time when you only know the average. Example: if 3 calls arrive per minute on average, how often will a minute bring 8 calls?

    A university help desk receives λ calls per minute on average, arriving at random. Staff can handle k calls in a minute. How often will a minute overload them?

    –minutes with more than k calls
    –overloaded minutes per hour

    Run a shift to compare real random minutes with the formula.

    Bars: Poisson probabilities. Red bars: overloaded minutes. Dots: your simulated shift.

    Where you see it

    Calls to a call centre, visitors to a website each second, accidents at a junction per month, typos per page, or goals in a football match.

    What to notice

    The mean and the variance are both λ. Small λ gives a lopsided shape with lots of zeros; large λ looks more and more like a bell.
    1.4

    Normal: the area under the bell is probability

    In simple words

    Many measurements, like height, cluster around an average and become rarer the further you go from it, making a bell shape. The area under the bell between two values is the chance of landing between them.

    Adult heights follow a bell curve with mean μ and spread σ. Pick a range of heights; the shaded area is the share of people inside it.

    –of people fall in this range

    Total area under the curve is 1, so the shaded area is a probability.

    Where you see it

    Clothing sizes, door heights, exam score scaling, measurement errors in a lab, and machine tolerances in factories are all planned around the normal curve.

    What to notice

    Whatever μ and σ are, about 68% of values sit within 1σ of the mean, 95% within 2σ and 99.7% within 3σ. The z-score tells you how many σ a value is from the mean.

    Lesson 2 of 3

    Sampling, the central limit theorem and confidence intervals

    We almost never see a whole population, only a sample. The central limit theorem explains why averages of samples behave so predictably, and confidence intervals turn that into an honest statement about what we don’t know.

    Missions

      2.1

      The central limit theorem machine

      In simple words

      Individual values can be messy or lopsided, but the averages of large enough samples always form a bell curve around the true average. That’s why we can trust averages even when we don’t know what the population looks like.

      Choose a population, even a lopsided one. Take a sample of size n, record its average, and repeat many times. Watch the shape of those averages.

      The population you are sampling from.

      0sample means
      –average of means
      –spread of means
      –CLT prediction σ/√n

      Where you see it

      Pollsters, factories and doctors rarely know the shape of the population. Because of the CLT they can still use the normal curve for averages of large enough samples.

      What to notice

      With n = 1 the means look exactly like the population. As n grows the means pile into a bell centred on μ, and the bell narrows like σ/√n.
      2.2

      The confidence interval factory

      In simple words

      One sample can’t tell us the exact truth, so we give a range instead: “the true average is probably between 4.6 and 5.6 hours.” A 95% interval means this method catches the truth about 95 times out of 100.

      Pretend we know the truth: students average μ = 5.0 hours of screen time a day, with σ = 1.5. A researcher who doesn’t know this takes one sample and builds an interval. How often does the interval catch the truth?

      Where you see it

      A poll that says “42% support, ±3%” is reporting a confidence interval. So is a lab result with an error bar, or a market survey estimating average spending.

      What to notice

      “95%” describes the method, not one interval: in the long run, 95% of intervals built this way catch μ. Higher confidence means wider intervals; bigger n means narrower ones.

      Lesson 3 of 3

      Hypothesis testing and linear regression

      A hypothesis test asks whether a pattern in data is real or just sampling luck. Regression goes further and describes how one variable moves with another, so you can make predictions.

      Missions

        3.1

        The juice bottle case

        In simple words

        A hypothesis test checks whether a claim still makes sense after looking at data. If the data would be very surprising when the claim is true (a small p-value), we reject the claim. Otherwise we don’t have enough evidence against it.

        A juice company claims its bottles hold 500 ml on average (σ = 10 ml). Like a court, we treat the claim as innocent, H₀: μ = 500, until the data give strong evidence against it, H₁: μ ≠ 500.

        Take a sample to start the test.The p-value is the chance of seeing a sample this extreme if H₀ were true.

        Standard normal curve under H₀. Red tails: rejection region of size α. Line: your z-statistic.

        Where you see it

        A/B tests on websites, drug trials, food safety audits and fraud checks all ask the same question: is this difference real, or could chance alone explain it?

        What to notice

        Rejecting a true H₀ is a Type I error (its rate is α). Missing a real difference is a Type II error. Bigger samples make real differences easier to detect.

        In simple words

        Regression draws the best straight line through data so you can predict one thing from another, like exam score from hours studied. The correlation r shows how closely the points follow that line: near +1 or −1 is tight, near 0 means no pattern.

        The line minimises the sum of squared residuals, so a single outlier can drag it. A strong r shows association, never causation on its own.
        3.3

        Guess the correlation

        In simple words

        This is practice for reading scatter plots. The more tightly the points hug a straight line, the closer r is to +1 (going up) or −1 (going down). A shapeless cloud has r near 0.

        Train your eye. Look at the cloud and guess its correlation coefficient, from −1 (perfect downhill line) to +1 (perfect uphill line).
